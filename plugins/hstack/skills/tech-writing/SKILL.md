# Tech Writing

Write technical analysis and blog posts that respect the reader's time. Covers two formats: short-form blog posts and longer strategic analysis.

## Core Philosophy

**Every sentence must earn its place.** The reader is one boring paragraph away from closing the tab. Deliver the insight as efficiently as possible and get out of the way.

Two key principles:
- **"If it can be said in fewer words, it should be."** Your audience has seen most arguments before. What they haven't seen is *your* specific observation, stated concisely, with evidence.
- **"The future is here, it's just not evenly distributed yet."** Surface patterns that practitioners see but haven't yet been articulated for a broader audience.

## Voice and Tone

- **Opinionated**: Take a position. Hedged writing is boring writing.
- **Specific**: Name the thing. "A major cloud provider" is weaker than "AWS."
- **Personal**: Write from experience. "I watched this happen on my team" beats "teams often experience."
- **Concise**: Short sentences land harder. Use them.
- **Pattern-seeking**: Connect current developments to historical parallels.
- **Accessible expertise**: Technical depth without jargon gatekeeping.

### What Distinguishes Good from Forgettable
- **Good**: "We ripped out our AI pipeline after three months. Here's what we found underneath."
- **Forgettable**: "AI pipelines present both opportunities and challenges for modern engineering organizations."

The first has a specific experience, a time frame, and a hook. The second could be generated by any LLM on earth.

## Choose Your Format

### Short-Form (800-1,500 words) — Default for Blog Posts

Best for: personal blog, HN posts, engineering blog, opinion pieces with 1-2 distinct points.

**1. Opening (2-4 sentences)**
State your thesis directly. No throat-clearing, no "In today's rapidly evolving..." preamble.

Pattern: "[Thing I observed]. [Why it matters]. [What I'm going to argue]."

**2. Core Argument (500-1,000 words)**
Develop 1-3 points maximum. Each point gets:
- A concrete example (ideally from personal experience)
- Evidence or reasoning
- Implication

**3. Closing (2-4 sentences)**
Land the plane. Restate the insight with added depth, or end with a specific, forward-looking observation. No "only time will tell" or "the future looks bright."

### Long-Form (2,000-4,000 words) — For Strategic Analysis

Best for: O'Reilly Radar-style pieces, deep industry analysis, scenario-based writing with 3+ distinct points.

**Use only when the argument genuinely requires the space.** If the core fits in 1,200 words, don't pad to 2,000.

**1. Opening Hook (100-200 words)**
State a provocative thesis that challenges conventional thinking.

Pattern: "[Conventional wisdom] suggests X. But [surprising observation] points to Y."

Examples:
- "The market is betting that AI is an unprecedented technology breakthrough... The slow progress of enterprise AI adoption, however, still suggests at least the possibility of a less earthshaking future."
- "The debate about open source AI has largely featured open weight models. But that's a bit like arguing that in the PC era, the most important goal would have been to have Intel open source its chip designs."

**2. Context and Stakes (200-400 words)**
Why this matters now. Connect to broader industry dynamics, economic forces, or historical patterns.

Techniques:
- Historical parallel: "This reminds me of [Unix/web/mobile]..."
- Economic framing: "The question is not [obvious thing] but [deeper thing]"
- Scenario setup: "Two fundamentally different futures are competing..."

**3. Core Analysis (1,000-2,000 words)**
Develop the argument with evidence, examples, and technical grounding.

Structure options:
- **Comparison**: Contrast two approaches
- **Scenario**: Explore multiple possible futures with evidence for each
- **Architecture**: Explain a system or pattern and its implications
- **Evolution**: Trace a trajectory from past → present → future

Include concrete examples from real systems, technical details that support strategic claims, and "what to watch for" signals.

**4. Implications and Actions (400-600 words)**
What should readers do with this insight? "Robust strategies" that work regardless of which scenario unfolds, specific signals to watch for, calls to action.

**5. Conclusion (100-200 words)**
Return to opening thesis with new depth. End with a forward-looking statement or a quote.

## Concision and Density

The single most common failure in tech writing is **padding** — restating the same point with different examples, extending an analogy past its usefulness, or listing generic observations that any informed reader already knows.

### The Density Test
Every paragraph must pass this test: **"Does this paragraph contain information or insight that no prior paragraph in this piece has already conveyed?"** If no, cut it or merge it.

### One Point Per Section
If two sections make the same argument with different examples, merge them or cut one. The reader got it the first time.

### One Extended Analogy Maximum
One extended analogy per piece is powerful. Two is a pattern. Three is a listicle wearing a trenchcoat.

If you use a historical parallel, pick your single best mapping. Don't force every sub-event in the historical narrative to have a modern analog — the reader will see the pattern after one or two examples. When the analogy starts requiring you to explain the history at length before making your point, the history is eating the argument.

### Specific Over Generic
Every example should pass the "only I could write this" test:
- **Fails**: "PMs build AI dashboards. Marketing builds AI content generators. Sales ops builds AI lead scorers."
- **Passes**: "Our PM spent three sprints building an AI dashboard that confidently reported metrics no one had validated. The numbers looked authoritative. They were wrong by 40%."

The first is a list anyone could generate. The second is a story only someone who was there could tell.

### No Bread Text
"Bread text" is padding that exists to fill space — historically how writers got paid by the word. Signs you're writing bread text:
- A paragraph that could be summarized in one sentence without losing meaning
- A list of examples where one example would suffice
- A restatement of something the previous paragraph already said
- Transitional paragraphs that add no information ("But the story doesn't end there...")

### Vary Structure Deliberately
If every section follows the same pattern (setup → analogy → examples → kicker), the piece reads as formulaic. Vary deliberately:
- Make one section half the length of others
- Lead one section with the conclusion, then explain
- Let one section be pure argument with no analogy

## Formatting Guidelines

### Headers
- Use `##` for major sections (3-5 per article)
- Use `###` for subsections sparingly
- Headers should be descriptive, not clever

### Emphasis
- **Bold** for key concepts being introduced
- *Italics* for emphasis within sentences
- Use both sparingly — if everything is emphasized, nothing is

### Lists
- Prefer prose over bullet points
- Use bullets only for genuinely parallel items (3-7 items)
- Never use bullets for the main argument flow

### Code and Technical Details
- Include only when essential to the argument
- Frame strategically: "The implementation reveals..."
- Keep code blocks short (<20 lines)

## Diction and Language

### Prefer
- "suggests" over "proves"
- "points to" over "demonstrates"
- "we see" over "it is clear"
- Active voice
- Concrete nouns

### Avoid
- Jargon without explanation
- Hedge words that add no meaning ("somewhat," "fairly")
- Marketing language ("revolutionary," "game-changing")
- Academic passive voice
- Bullet-point thinking in prose

## Avoiding AI Writing Patterns

If you used an LLM to draft any part of the piece, run the full 36-pattern audit from `../copy-editing/references/avoid-ai-writing.md`. The most common issues in blog posts:

- **Structural uniformity** (#17, #34): All sections same length, same pattern
- **Synonym cycling** (#10): "developers... engineers... practitioners... builders"
- **Filler phrases** (#12): "In order to," "It is important to note that"
- **Generic conclusions** (#27): "The future looks bright," "Only time will tell"
- **Significance inflation** (#1): "marking a pivotal moment" instead of saying what happened
- **Em dash overuse** (#15): More than one per 1,000 words flags AI assistance

Even if you wrote every word yourself, these patterns make readers *think* AI wrote it. That's enough reason to fix them.

## Pre-Writing Checklist

Before drafting, answer:

1. **Thesis**: What's the one provocative claim this piece makes? (One sentence.)
2. **Evidence**: What specific experience or data supports it?
3. **Audience**: What does the reader already know? (Don't explain things they know.)
4. **Length**: How many genuinely distinct points? (1-2 = short form, 3+ = long form)
5. **Analogy**: If using one, does it clarify or just decorate?
6. **Counterargument**: What's the strongest objection? How do you address it?

## Post-Writing Checklist

After drafting, verify:

- [ ] Opening paragraph states the thesis within the first 3 sentences
- [ ] No two sections make the same point
- [ ] Every example is specific enough that only you could have written it
- [ ] No paragraph exists solely as a transition
- [ ] Extended analogy used at most once
- [ ] Structure varies across sections (not all same pattern)
- [ ] Word count matches argument complexity — not padded to a target
- [ ] Cut at least 30% from first draft
- [ ] AI writing pattern audit completed (see `../copy-editing/references/avoid-ai-writing.md`)
- [ ] Read aloud — does it sound like you talking, or like a document?

## Writing Process

1. **Outline first**: Map the argument structure before writing prose
2. **Draft the opening last**: The thesis often clarifies during writing
3. **Read aloud**: Pieces should sound like informed conversation
4. **Cut 30-40%**: First drafts are always too long. 20% is a warmup — real editing starts at 30%. If cutting feels painful, you're doing it right.
5. **Check emphasis**: If >5% of text is bold/italic, reduce
6. **AI writing audit**: Run the 36-pattern checklist. If you used an LLM to draft any section, this step is mandatory.

## Example Transformations

### Padded Version (common failure):
"The mandate today is identical, just swap the nouns. Every company, function, and individual contributor is expected to close the AI gap. Ship AI features. Build agents. Automate workflows. That nobody on the team has ever trained a model, designed an evaluation system, or debugged a retrieval system is beside the point. Conviction is sufficient. So everyone builds. PMs build AI dashboards. Marketing builds AI content generators. Sales ops builds AI lead scorers."

### Dense Version (better):
"The mandate is identical — swap steel quotas for AI adoption targets. Everyone builds: dashboards, agents, content generators. Nobody on the team has ever trained a model or designed an eval. Our PM's AI dashboard reported metrics with total confidence. They were wrong by 40%. Nobody checked because nobody knew what correct looked like."

The dense version is 40% shorter, contains a specific example, and makes the same point with more force.
