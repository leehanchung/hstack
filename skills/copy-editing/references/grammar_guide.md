## 1. Parts of Speech

### Nouns
- **Common vs. Proper**: Common nouns are generic (city, company); proper nouns are specific (Chicago, Microsoft)
- **Collective Nouns**: Treat as singular in American English (The team is winning), plural in British English (The team are winning)
- **Countable vs. Uncountable**: Affects article usage (a book vs. some information)

### Verbs
- **Action vs. Linking**: Action verbs show activity; linking verbs connect subject to complement
- **Transitive vs. Intransitive**: Transitive requires object; intransitive doesn't
- **Modal Verbs**: Can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would

### Adjectives & Adverbs
- **Comparative Forms**: -er/-est for short words (faster, fastest); more/most for longer words (more beautiful, most beautiful)
- **Adjective Order**: Opinion, size, age, shape, color, origin, material, purpose (beautiful large old round red Italian leather riding boots)
- **Adverb Placement**: Generally flexible but avoid splitting infinitives unnecessarily

## 2. Sentence Structure

### Simple Sentences
- Subject + Verb (+ Object/Complement)
- Example: "The manager approved the proposal."

### Compound Sentences
- Two independent clauses joined by coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so)
- Example: "The report was thorough, but it arrived late."

### Complex Sentences
- Independent clause + dependent clause
- Example: "Although the report was thorough, it arrived after the deadline."

### Compound-Complex Sentences
- Multiple independent clauses + at least one dependent clause
- Example: "When the report arrived, the manager reviewed it, but the team had already moved forward."

## 3. Agreement Rules

### Subject-Verb Agreement
- Singular subjects take singular verbs; plural subjects take plural verbs
- Intervening phrases don't affect agreement: "The box of cookies is on the table"
- Compound subjects joined by "and" are plural: "Tom and Jerry are friends"
- Subjects joined by "or/nor" agree with closer subject: "Neither the students nor the teacher was prepared"

### Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement
- Pronouns must agree in number, gender, and person
- Indefinite pronouns (everyone, someone) are singular: "Everyone brought his or her lunch" or "Everyone brought their lunch" (singular they)
- Collective nouns can be singular or plural depending on context

## 4. Verb Forms and Tenses

### Present Tenses
- **Simple Present**: Habitual actions, general truths
- **Present Progressive**: Actions happening now
- **Present Perfect**: Past actions with present relevance
- **Present Perfect Progressive**: Ongoing actions started in past

### Past Tenses
- **Simple Past**: Completed actions in past
- **Past Progressive**: Ongoing actions in past
- **Past Perfect**: Action completed before another past action
- **Past Perfect Progressive**: Ongoing action completed before past point

### Future Tenses
- **Simple Future**: Will + base verb
- **Future Progressive**: Will be + -ing
- **Future Perfect**: Will have + past participle
- **Future Perfect Progressive**: Will have been + -ing

### Subjunctive Mood
- Contrary-to-fact conditions: "If I were you..."
- Demands/suggestions: "She insisted that he be present"

## 5. Pronoun Usage

### Personal Pronouns
- **Subjective**: I, you, he, she, it, we, they
- **Objective**: me, you, him, her, it, us, them
- **Possessive**: my/mine, your/yours, his, her/hers, its, our/ours, their/theirs

### Common Errors
- **Who vs. Whom**: Who = subject; whom = object
- **Its vs. It's**: Its = possessive; it's = it is/it has
- **They're, Their, There**: They're = they are; their = possessive; there = location

### Pronoun Reference
- Must clearly refer to specific antecedent
- Avoid vague "it," "this," "that" without clear reference
- Watch for ambiguous references with multiple possible antecedents

## 6. Modifier Placement

### Misplaced Modifiers
- Place modifiers near words they modify
- Wrong: "She served sandwiches to the children on paper plates"
- Right: "She served sandwiches on paper plates to the children"

### Dangling Modifiers
- Modifier must have clear subject to modify
- Wrong: "Walking down the street, the trees were beautiful"
- Right: "Walking down the street, I noticed the beautiful trees"

### Limiting Modifiers
- Only, just, merely, almost, nearly, even
- Position affects meaning: "Only she ate cake" vs. "She only ate cake" vs. "She ate only cake"

## 7. Punctuation Rules

### Commas
- Between independent clauses with conjunction
- After introductory elements
- Around nonrestrictive elements
- Between items in series (including Oxford comma)
- Between coordinate adjectives

### Semicolons
- Between independent clauses without conjunction
- Between items in complex lists
- Before conjunctive adverbs (however, therefore, moreover)

### Colons
- Introduce lists, explanations, or quotes
- After complete sentence only
- Capitalize after colon if complete sentence follows (style dependent)

### Apostrophes
- Possession: Add 's to singular (dog's), ' to plural ending in s (dogs')
- Contractions: Replace missing letters (don't, it's, '90s)
- Never for plural: CDs, 1990s (not CD's, 1990's)

### Quotation Marks
- Direct quotes and dialogue
- Titles of short works
- Words used ironically or as words
- Periods and commas inside; colons and semicolons outside (American style)

## 8. Common Grammar Pitfalls

### Affect vs. Effect
- Affect = verb (to influence)
- Effect = noun (result) or verb (to cause)

### Lie vs. Lay
- Lie = intransitive (lie down)
- Lay = transitive (lay something down)

### Fewer vs. Less
- Fewer = countable items
- Less = uncountable quantities

### That vs. Which
- That = restrictive (essential) clauses, no commas
- Which = nonrestrictive (nonessential) clauses, with commas

### Between vs. Among
- Between = two items or distinct items
- Among = three or more non-distinct items

### Parallel Structure
- Items in lists must be grammatically parallel
- Wrong: "She likes swimming, to run, and biking"
- Right: "She likes swimming, running, and biking"
